PhD in Economics

Multinational Enterprises and the Transmission of the Global Financial Cycle: A Firm-Level Analysis for Germany — provisional working title

The dissertation examines how global financial conditions transmit to the real economy through individual firms, and what role multinational enterprises play in that transmission. Global risk aversion, funding costs and financial uncertainty are known to move capital flows and real activity across countries, but the microeconomic channels through which they reach firm-level productivity, investment and employment remain incompletely understood. Multinationals are central to this question in two opposing ways: their international production and financing structures may expose them more directly to global shocks, while internal capital markets and diversified revenue streams may instead make them more resilient. The empirical core uses confidential AFiD firm-level microdata for Germany, which cover private as well as listed firms and so avoid the selection problems of listed-firm samples, and which contain the quantity and price information needed to estimate physical total factor productivity (TFPQ) — separating genuine efficiency changes from price and demand effects. The strategy proceeds through firm-level production function estimation, panel regressions with firm and time fixed effects, and heterogeneity analysis comparing multinational with purely domestic firms and firms under differing degrees of financial constraint. Germany is a well-suited case given its export orientation, the weight of multinationals in its economy, and its position in European production networks.

Estimating Differential Routing Rates in Germany's Federal Employment Agency: An Epidemiological Approach to AI Incident Monitoring in High-Risk Labour-Market Systems With Aileen Voit, Nafisah Animashaun and Leonard Schmidt · SAIGE Incubator Working Paper, 2026 · Mentor: Branwen Nia Owen (Arcadia)

Germany's Federal Employment Agency (Bundesagentur für Arbeit, BA) uses algorithmic profiling to route unemployed jobseekers into support tracks that differ in intensity and duration. Advocacy organisations have long suspected that refugees and migrants are directed disproportionately into low-support tracks, but no systematic estimate of the disparity existed. We treat the question as a problem of administrative surveillance: a structured monitoring question fixes the scope, exposure and harm are estimated separately from published statistics, and the case is placed on a four-category harm trajectory. Using BA monthly data on jobseekers in the context of flight migration for June 2016 to April 2026, we measure an exposed population of 776,000 registered jobseekers and compare their registration outcomes with those of other third-country nationals. The disparity proves specific to one legal regime. In the basic-income system (SGB II), which administers 89 per cent of flight-context jobseekers, the two groups are registered for occupational tiers at the same rate. In the unemployment-insurance system (SGB III), which jobseekers reach only through prior insured employment and where the profiling infrastructure operates, flight-context jobseekers are registered for unskilled-tier occupations 1.6 times as often — a gap of 25 percentage points that holds across all 16 federal states and survives worst-case bounds on education composition. The same group receives substantive vocational training at half the comparison rate. Because the two regimes select structurally different populations, published data cannot separate the profiling tool from admission-channel selection or caseworker practice: what the data establish is where the disparity sits rather than what produces it. That opacity is itself a finding — no methodology for the system has ever been published, placing it in anticipated breach of §35a VwVfG and Article 13 of the EU AI Act ahead of the August 2026 high-risk applicability deadline. The case is characteristic of the chronic, bureaucratic, non-Anglophone harms that AI incident databases rarely record, and the monitoring approach carries over to other public-sector systems.

Predicting the Price of Safety: AI Governance Exposure and Venture Financing Sole-authored · Draft, 2026

This project examines whether AI governance and safety considerations shape venture financing outcomes for AI and deeptech startups. Using May 2025 Crunchbase data, I construct linked firm-round, investor-edge, partner-edge, difference-in-differences-ready and machine-learning-ready panels to study financing dynamics around major AI governance and market-salience shocks, including the EU AI Act trilogue and the release of ChatGPT. The paper develops a distinction between explicit governance disclosure and broader governance exposure, the latter capturing AI and deeptech startups operating in high-risk or compliance-sensitive domains such as healthcare, finance, cybersecurity, biometrics, hiring, education and autonomous systems. Preliminary event-study evidence suggests no immediate funding response after the AI Act trilogue, consistent with delayed investor learning or repricing among governance-exposed startups already visible to venture capital markets. The project contributes to entrepreneurial finance, AI economics, and empirical work using text-based and network-based measures of startup characteristics.
